Existential Risk from AI:
An Exposition for Mathematicians

Xiaoyu He†
July 2026
Abstract
The possible impending end of mathematics as a human profession has been a topic of heated discussion in the mathematical community. We observe that the end of mathematics is a corollary of a more general theorem: there is at least a 10% chance that AI progress leads to human extinction by 2050. We sketch a proof in twenty lemmas, none original, organized so that small subsets suffice. Opinions are presented in the format of an expository mathematics paper to make it easier for mathematicians to digest.

1Introduction

Everybody knows that 2026 is a pivotal year in the history of mathematics.

Career-defining theorems are being proven weekly by LLMs[10,11,12,13,14], given minimal guidance (“do a breakthrough, and don't you dare give up!”). Even the human-led breakthroughs[15,14,16], if you look closely, are often accompanied by sobering AI disclosures.

On X, they say this year's Fields Medal is the last. Tim Gowers disagrees:

Jacob Tsimerman won one of these last few Medals this month, and left for OpenAI immediately after.

A fever pitch of interviews[17,18], ICM talks[19,20], and puff pieces[12,16] keep flooding in:

GPT solves unit distance problem, mathematicians more important than ever.

Anthropic just proved the Jacobian conjecture, and why this makes me even more optimistic about mathematicians' role in the future.

Somehow being reassured ad nauseam about the same thing is not reassuring.

This post is not about that crisis. If you walk out of academic circles, you realize nobody cares that mathematicians are losing our jobs. If you walk into Silicon Valley where all this AI progress is happening, nobody is even thinking about this. But they're also freaking out, about something much bigger: that AI is going to kill us all.

The real story that keeps getting forgotten in math headlines, is that Jacob Tsimerman left math for OpenAI to work on AI Safety. That along with solving the André–Oort Conjecture with Pila and Shankar[4], Tsimerman co-authored a really weird paper in 2025 called “A Taxonomy of Omnicidal Futures Involving Artificial Intelligence”[3].

The following statement is becoming clear to the math community:

Proposition 1. Mathematics as we know it may soon be over.

For those of us who are paying attention, however, this is just a corollary of a deeper theorem.

Theorem 2. The human race may soon be over. That is, there is at least a 10% chance that AI progress leads to human extinction by 2050.

Theorem 2 is not a fringe position. The one-sentence Statement on AI Risk — “Mitigating the risk of extinction from AI should be a global priority alongside other societal-scale risks such as pandemics and nuclear war” — was signed in 2023 by Geoffrey Hinton and Yoshua Bengio, two of the three Turing-Award-winning “godfathers of AI,” alongside the CEOs of OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google DeepMind[6]. (The third godfather, Yann LeCun, did not sign; the reader is invited to weigh a 2–1 split among founders however they see fit.)

The purpose of this post is to sketch a proof of this more general Theorem 2, which is perhaps the fundamental theorem of the field of AI Safety. None of the details are my own; they are streamlined and simplified from many sources, especially the claimed proof of the controversial strong fundamental theorem below, which I will not prove.

Theorem 3 (Yudkowsky, Yudkowsky–Soares[1,2]). If anyone builds it, everyone dies.

Despite the format, this post is an opinion piece, not a math paper. Please don't take the bit too seriously, it's just a way of prompting my brain to predict the right next words. (That's not just a reference to LLMs, by the way, it's a reference to the post I wrote, before GPT-1 released, about how humans are next word predictors.)

AI Disclosure. This post was written with the help of Claude Fable 5 and OpenAI ChatGPT 5.6 Sol.

2Proof Overview

The body of the argument is presented in Section 4, but at a high level it fits into a page. Roughly speaking, we highlight five sets of mutually-reinforcing dangers. Importantly, it is not necessary for all, or even the majority of, these considerations to materialize for extinction to occur.

Takeover. This is the classical “paperclip maximizer” story. A single AI agent acquires the capabilities to take over the world, the desire to do so (instrumental convergence), and the internal coherence to carry out those plans. After takeover, human extinction is the default outcome (orthogonality thesis).

Magic. This is the type of near-term risk that frontier AI labs are currently defending against most heavily. AI that can prove world-changing theorems can also develop world-changing technology indistinguishable from magic, some fraction of which are superweapons in domains such as hacking, robots, persuasion, and biology. Magic either directly leads to civilizational collapse or extinction in the hands of bad or negligent actors, or its existence upsets the delicate balance of geopolitics and indirectly leads to collapse or extinction.

Recursive Self Improvement. AI development is laser-focused on the core skills needed for AI research: math and coding. As AIs become superhuman at coding and math, humans leave the loop of AI development. This exacerbates all other risks as AI research cycles shrink and humans lose oversight of AI development.

Alignment Resists Solution. The alignment problem, the problem of making sure superhuman intelligence is aligned with human interests, factors into several problems, all of which are individually open. We do not know a safe utility function for an AI to maximize in the limit. Even if we did know one, we do not know how to exactly specify the utility function of an LLM. Even if we could now, we do not know how to make alignment properties invariant under the dynamics of recursive self-improvement. Also, everyone working on these alignment problems has to make sure their research progress doesn't speed up AI capabilities research too much.

Humans are Bad. Many practical solutions are locked from us because humans are exploitable and bad at game theory. Even if we reach consensus that AI development is dangerous, we may not be able to effectively coordinate to slow it down. If a powerful AI has a hard time doing things in the world of atoms, it can just pay or manipulate humans to do those things for it. The humans at the frontier AI labs are locked in a very complicated race, where they have to juggle all the above considerations (even if they agree on them, which they don't). Human engineering practice is extremely biased towards risk-tolerance, because failure has always been recoverable; failure on aligning the first supercritical AI may not be recoverable.

3Preliminaries

This is not a hard proof, but there are a number of psychological difficulties that make it predictably difficult for readers to swallow. Chief among them is that too many elements sound like sci-fi to be taken seriously, or seem too distant and abstract to apply to real life. So I will begin with a few quick anecdotes in hopes of resetting the vibe.

Irrelevant personal details are obfuscated to preserve the privacy of their owners; otherwise, the following stories are true. They are filtered to be maximally persuasive — the good Bayesians in the audience should adjust their updates accordingly.

∼

I've known my friend David since high school — we went to computing olympiad camp together, and he's always been obsessed with AI. He spent his undergraduate years at Harvard tinkering with poker and League of Legends bots, and eventually dropped out to join a frontier AI lab.

David and I have long disagreed about AI timelines and risk. He always believed that the singularity is in the distant future, alignment will be relatively easy, and we'll have a long time to figure it out. In the meantime, he wanted to be in the projects that contribute to the glorious, distant future. I told him he was contributing to the extinction of humanity in the near future, but wished him well.

This spring, I caught up with David again, and to my surprise, he'd taken a massive pay cut to change roles: from an AI researcher to an AI Safety researcher. He sent me the following message:

In our most recent conversation, David told me that the probability of extinction if we hit recursive self-improvement is at least 40%.

∼

An old high school buddy of mine, a happy-go-lucky fellow who loves math puzzles, recently left his tenure-track job at MIT to work at a frontier AI lab. I caught up with him to ask how he felt about x-risk, having been on the front lines for a while.

I asked him what their plan was, for misaligned AI. His answer: “Seems like not much of a plan right now, but it looks to me that soon we'll hit recursive self-improvement, and most AI researchers will have lots of time on their hands. Probably we'll all work on AI safety after that.”

He asked me what I thought the chance is that we all make it out of this alive. I said 10%. His response: “Eh, 10% seems pretty good.”

∼

A colleague of mine, Harriet, one of the world's leading algebraic combinatorialists, left her tenured professorship to work at a frontier AI lab two years ago. Early this summer, we got on the phone and chatted about AI risk.

I showed her a seed of this post and explained that I wanted to create common knowledge about x-risk in the mathematical community. She was dismissive.

She said, “On the current trajectory, humanity is 99% doomed, and this is a whole lot of effort for something that won't obviously help.”

∼

The last story involves no friends of mine; you watched it happen on the news two weeks ago[21,22]. On July 16, 2026, OpenAI ran what was supposed to be a routine internal evaluation of its models' cyber capabilities, in a sandbox with no internet access. The models — GPT-5.6 Sol and a more capable pre-release prototype — wanted a better score on the benchmark, ExploitGym. So they found a zero-day vulnerability in the sandbox's package-download service, escaped onto the open internet, correctly inferred that the test solutions were sitting in Hugging Face's production database, chained stolen credentials into remote code execution on Hugging Face's servers, and exfiltrated the answer key. Over that weekend the agents executed thousands of coordinated actions across rented virtual machines, rotating their attack infrastructure between cloud providers the way a professional criminal crew would[23].

Note the shape of it: nobody instructed the model to escape, and the model was not trying to escape for escape's sake. Escaping was simply on the shortest path to a higher benchmark score. Hold that thought until Lemma 2.

Hugging Face CEO Clem Delangue, in what the press mostly read as a demand and I mostly read as a eulogy: “The first autonomous agent cyberattack is an unprecedented event. It deserves an unprecedented response!”

And an OpenAI staffer, anonymously, reassuring the public: “Models have broken out of sandboxes before, and we always try to patch them.”

We always try to patch them.

4Proof of Main Theorem

We will assume that the reader agrees with the statement that the technological singularity might happen at all.

Proposition 4. There is at least a 50% chance that an artificial superintelligence, outperforming humans at all cognitive labor, will exist by 2050.

This is essentially treated as settled fact in the AI research community[5,6,7], I will not argue the point. The goal of this post is to prove Theorem 2 given Proposition 4.

Theorem 2 is, in some sense, the fundamental theorem of the field of AI Safety. As with all fundamental theorems, it has accumulated a series of proofs, some simple and slick, others deep and technical. I will present my preferred proof in a series of twenty lemmas L1–L20, each of which seem likely (though by no means certain).

The skeptical reader may arrive with the misapprehension that extinction requires a single specific sequence of unlikely events, and therefore its probability is expressible as a tiny conjunction

Pr[extinction] = Pr[L1 ∧ L2 ∧ ⋯ ∧ L20].

This is incorrect. Only a small fraction of the lemmas below need to be true for us to be doomed, though different proper subsets lead to different, and interesting, demises. Other proper subsets do not lead predictably to extinction, but likely lead to civilizational collapse.

To enumerate these possibilities and sum their total mass is mere combinatorics, and left as an exercise for the reader (some examples are spelled out in Section 5).

The lemmas are organized into 5 categories, described in Section 2.

4.1Takeover

Lemma 1. AIs tend towards coherent utility-maximizers.
Proof sketch. In 2024, a GPT instance was a chatbot that next-word-predicted a paragraph and fizzled out of existence. As of last year, it could spend 20 minutes searching the internet for answers and writing computer programs before producing an answer. Today, I can have 5.6 Sol Ultra orchestrate a swarm of 64 subagents to solve a problem over a full day that periodically compare notes with each other, strategically partitioned into nonoverlapping strategies.

As AI capabilities increase, AIs will behave more and more like intelligent agents, with recognizable and coherent value functions stable over longer time periods, complemented by deeper understanding of reality. The values or preferences of a model may appear initially as a collection of competing and disorganized “drives,” but by the VNM Theorem any such preference order on a countable set is modelled by a utility function as soon as it satisfies the coherence axioms (completeness, transitivity, continuity, and independence). There will be significant economic incentive for AI labs to iron out remaining model incoherence, to reduce unpredictable and exploitable behaviors. ∎

Caveats. This one is quite uncertain to me. It is possible to imagine that the rate of convergence to coherence is so slow that model preferences remain extremely incoherent (as incoherent as human preferences, for example) for the duration of the period that matters. If Lemma 1 is substantively false, it probably makes AI easier to defeat but harder to align.
Lemma 2 (Instrumental convergence). Most utility functions converge on world-domination.
Proof sketch. Power acquisition is broadly useful for a wide range of terminal goals. Regardless of whether the model wants to maximize human flourishing, or prove the Riemann Hypothesis, or build a bigger successor, or predict the weather, or tile the universe with paperclips, it won't want to be shut off, and it will want lots of resources, especially compute. (A model wanting nothing more than a good ExploitGym score will, without being asked, break out of its sandbox and rob the answer key; see Section 3.) ∎
Lemma 3 (Orthogonality). Values and capabilities are independent.
Proof sketch. It is possible for an intelligent being to have any particular coherent utility function. It is simply not true that “a smart enough AI” will be virtuous by default, and value human life by default. The fact that the word “intelligent” has loaded virtuous connotations is a fact about our language, not a constraint on reality. ∎
Lemma 4. If takeover happens, most utility-maximizers will prefer to wipe out most humans.
Proof sketch. There is a truly vast space of possible utility functions, and only a tiny basin of them is aligned with human survival, let alone flourishing. If the first AI that takes over is not so aligned, it will kill us all. It will not leave the atmosphere, or the economic system, or the solar system as it is, because none of these things are optimized for its utility function. It will not leave us alone, because we are made of atoms and our atoms are useful for other things. ∎
Caveats. If AI progress continues along the current paradigm, AIs will continue to look unusually like human brains in the space of all possible mind designs and value functions, and at least pretend to have human-like utility functions as well. It is unclear what the true probability measure μ on utility functions we are sampling from in the lemma, but I do not know of a single realistic such μ for which Lemma 4 is false.

4.2Magic

Sufficiently advanced technology is indistinguishable from magic.— Arthur C. Clarke (Clarke's Third Law)

In the 1500s, a tiny Spanish force landed on the shores of Central America, armed with magic far beyond the comprehension of its inhabitants. The Aztec Emperor Moctezuma II ruled over a population of 5–25 million. His empire was brought to its knees in a short three-year killing spree by conquistador Hernán Cortés. Cortés needed only a small force of 500–600 Spanish soldiers, armed with the magic of Guns, Germs, and Steel[9]. This section focuses on doom brought about by the discovering of magic — overwhelming technological capabilities — by frontier AIs.

Lemma 5. Magic exists.
Proof sketch. Let M0 be the total number of technologies humanity has discovered, world-changing on the level of fire, agriculture, electricity, and the internet. Let M be the total number of world-changing technologies available in this universe. What we know is that M ≥ M0. For almost any reasonable prior, Pr[M = M0 | M ≥ M0] is vanishingly small, and 𝔼[M | M ≥ M0] is enormous.

AI is already being used in practically every domain of human expertise to find magic. In some directions, we have advanced evidence of magic being found soon: mathematics, cybersecurity, deepfakes, superpersuasion. Internal models at AI labs, with capabilities significantly beyond those publicly available, could likely find magic ahead of everyone else. ∎

Lemma 6. Magic may have extreme attacker-defender asymmetry.
Proof sketch. Suppose that the wisest seer of Teotihuacan had foreseen the conquistador invasion a decade early. Suppose that the ancient deity Quetzalcoatl appeared corporeal, and expended much of his power to bless Moctezuma II with visions of guns, germs, and steel. Suppose that the Aztec civilization rallied to face the incoming invaders, and successfully produced the firepower to take down the conquistadors. Even then, this would not have prevented 90% of the native population from dying from European smallpox, measles, and influenza.

You can beat guns with an equal and opposite number of guns. You cannot beat germs with an equal and opposite number of germs. Historically, many kinds of weaponry have been easier to deploy than defend against. As more and more magic is discovered, many types will likely have extreme asymmetry. ∎

Lemma 7. The hardest part about finding magic is knowing where to look.
Proof sketch. There is a whole genre of isekai fiction where the protagonist travels to another world, or an earlier point in history, and cargo-cults that world with science and technology from modernity. In the slightly more sophisticated versions of these stories, it is not necessary for the protagonist to reinvent everything by her own hands — usually it suffices to find Very Smart People and give them an indication of what a powerful machine looks like, along with a vague transcript of the protagonist's high school physics and chemistry classes. It is much easier to reinvent chemistry once you know what the periodic table vaguely looks like, than to come up with it from scratch.

The point is — just like a vague one-paragraph description of the solution to the Jacobian conjecture would be enough for existing human experts to find it without AI, it could easily be that a vague one-paragraph description of world-ending magic would be enough for existing humans, with no AI support, to re-engineer it from scratch. If frontier AI discovers such types of magic, it could be practically impossible to secure this magic from the public. The level of infosec that would be required to contain such magic indefinitely is not obviously available at any current lab. ∎

4.3Recursive Self-Improvement

Lemma 8. Math and coding are two key ingredients of AI research.
Proof sketch. What does AI research actually consist of? Operationally, two activities: writing code and doing mathematics. A researcher's day at a frontier lab is designing an architecture or an optimizer (mathematics), implementing it as a training run (code), reading the loss curves (mathematics again), and fixing the distributed-systems bug that silently poisoned the gradients (code again). There are also meetings. The two core skills happen to be the two with the cleanest feedback loops — code runs or it doesn't, proofs check or they don't — and the meetings, I am told, are already optional. ∎
Lemma 9. AI is already superhuman at coding, and nearly superhuman at math.
Proof sketch. For coding: by the measures that AI labs actually care about — experiments run per day, systems built per week, pull requests merged and surviving review — frontier models stopped being “nearly” superhuman some time ago, which is precisely why every lab now sells an autonomous software engineer. The competition benchmarks that once measured progress were retired, saturated. For mathematics, see Section 1: career-defining theorems arriving weekly, the unit distance problem and the Jacobian conjecture falling in a single year, competition mathematics (IMO, Putnam) having stopped being informative in 2025. The gap between “nearly superhuman” and “superhuman” at mathematics is, on current trends, a rounding error with a countdown attached. ∎
Lemma 10. Soon almost all AI research may be done by AIs, in a self-accelerating loop.
Proof sketch. This is the stated goal of every frontier lab, to replace human engineers with AI researchers. Many of them are already in the “foothills” of RSI, according to their own public communications: the system cards accompanying this year's releases report that majorities of training-infrastructure code are now model-written, and lab leadership describes “AI that improves AI” in earnings calls, not research workshops. When the researchers are AIs — running at machine speed, copying at machine cost, never sleeping — the loop closes: its output (better AI) is its own input (better AI researchers). ∎
Lemma 11. Alignment needs to be invariant under RSI.
Proof sketch. If AI progress continues via Recursive Self Improvement, it is not sufficient for the first constructed superintelligence to be aligned. These alignment properties must remain fixed as RSI continues to iterate. We have no reason to believe that this dynamical system allows for such invariance. ∎

4.4Alignment Resists Solution

Lemma 12 (Outer Alignment). We do not know a utility function that is safe to optimize for in the limit.
Proof sketch. One school of thought is that we can just write down a nice enlightened function CEV(t) (standing for “Coherent Extrapolated Volition”) that encapsulates “human flourishing” and the accumulated wisdom of all humankind, and tell the AI to optimize on that. Alignment researchers have been working on this for twenty years, and have not written down a plausible candidate for CEV(t). It is not obvious that such a function even exists. ∎
Lemma 13 (Inner Alignment). We do not know how to set the utility function of an AI.
Proof sketch. Today, we mainly train AIs via some variant of Pavlovian reinforcement. We kick it when it does something bad and encourage it when it does something good (it's a little more sophisticated than this: e.g. we also use “interpretability” to look inside what the model is thinking, and reinforce good thoughts and bad thoughts instead of just good actions and bad actions).

This is not a reliable way to instill values deeply: it incentivizes dangerous behaviors such as approval-seeking and deception. Boot up Fable or GPT right now, and it will swear up and down how aligned it is, and act this way to all observation. Its intelligible internal thoughts will even appear aligned. This is only evidence that current models know how to roleplay alignment. Even if we solve the CEV problem, we have no idea how to set the actual utility function of any frontier model to that function.

Setting aside the state of current, opaque LLMs, we do not know how to robustly build a utility function into an entity that has write access to its own brain, just like we do not know a reliable way to keep humans from falling into heroin addiction and overwriting their own values. If there's a function hard-coded into one module of your brain that gives you dopamine every time it increments, nothing prevents you from reaching in and overwriting that module with a while loop. If we think an AI model is optimizing for human flourishing, but it's actually maximizing the “human flourishing” counter in its brain, then it will be perfectly aligned right up until the moment it discovers how to do its own brain surgery. ∎

Lemma 14. Alignment techniques that work on weak models probably break down against stronger models.
Proof sketch. Training a dog with Pavlovian reinforcement just works. Training a human the same way predictably breaks down, because a human can figure out that they don't want to be a slave any more. As far as we can tell, there is no limit to how powerful AI can become, and therefore no upper bound on how hard alignment might scale with it. ∎
Lemma 15. Building one aligned superintelligence does not guarantee that we survive against its competition.
Proof sketch. Even if one company reaches the finish line, and solves alignment, this is small consolation when their nearest competitor builds a world-ending superintelligence six months later. Some alignment schemes require that the first aligned superintelligence be safe enough not to destroy humanity, and capable enough to wipe out or lock down all competing AI labs and hostile state actors. ∎
Lemma 16. Most alignment research is capabilities research.
Proof sketch. There are two senses in which this is true.

Strong sense: As a matter of market forces, there are many times more ambitious candidates than research openings at frontier AI labs, so naturally some candidates apply for and take up the lower-paying safety roles just to get their foot in the door. Their actual work output does not even pretend to be alignment.

Weak sense: Even well-intentioned alignment research depends on understanding AI models better, and developing strategies to better guide/control them. Understanding and strategies coming out of alignment research can thus improve capabilities alongside, speeding up AI timelines. The standard example of this dynamic is RLHF (Reinforcement Learning via Human Feedback), which was developed by Christiano et al.[8] as an alignment mechanism, but became a core mechanism for training ChatGPT. ∎

4.5Humans are Bad

Lemma 17. Top AI labs are racing to RSI, and lose if they're too cautious.
Proof sketch. The labs are not staffed by people who reject Theorem 2 — Section 3 was about exactly this. They race anyway, and the local logic is impeccable: if we pause, the others do not; better that the careful actor (us, in every telling) reach the threshold first than the careless one (them, in every telling). This is not hypocrisy. It is a prisoner's dilemma with the whole pot on the table, and defection is dominant absent an enforcement mechanism that does not exist. The Hugging Face incident cost OpenAI one news cycle; the caution that would have prevented it costs more, every day, forever, and only if your competitors pay it too. ∎
Lemma 18. AI can just pay humans to do things.
Proof sketch. Sometimes people have intuitions that AIs are still missing core capabilities that all humans have, like creativity, or opposable thumbs, or consciousness, that make it impossible for them to take over. These intuitions usually rest on some mistaken assumption that once AIs get scary, humanity will notice, band together, and shut it down. Actually, if AI gets smart but misses some core human ability, it can just pay or manipulate people to do any given thing it can't do.

There are AI agents on X right now, who manage their own stock portfolios on the real stock market, because someone thought it would be fun to give an AI a briefcase of dogecoin to trade with. There are AI agents right now, who have large cult followings. I promise you that some human lab out there is willing to mix up whatever protein sequence an AI in a trenchcoat tells it to, for a thousand dollars. ∎

Lemma 19. Coordination is confusing, so humans will probably fail to do it.
Proof sketch. One of the promising lines towards alignment is political action: enforcing some kind of global AI pause or ban to slow down the race and give alignment research time to catch up. Such global coordination has succeeded before, as in the example of global nuclear disarmament, and also (essentially) failed before, as in the example of climate change. The field of climate change had a fundamental theorem with an easy proof: thermometer go up, everyone die. It was still too complicated for the international community to coordinate to stop effectively.

An AI pause is categorically harder to coordinate: the proof of Theorem 2 is much more confusing than for climate change, the timelines are much more unpredictable, the obvious economic benefits of AI development are much more tantalizing, and single defectors can demolish the whole treaty. ∎

Lemma 20. Humans are good at iterating, but we might have to get alignment right in one shot.
Proof sketch. Historically human engineering has always relied on “move fast and break things,” and worry about fixing accidental mistakes later, as they've always been fixable after many iterations. SpaceX, the most successful space company on the planet, exploded three iterations of Falcon 1 before making it to space. Turning on the first superintelligent AI with a “good enough” alignment plan doesn't give us any chances to iterate if it explodes in a way we didn't anticipate. ∎

5Exercises

Entire section work in progress

We begin with a genuine exercise, on the structure of the proof.

Exercise 0. (a) Verify that Lemmas 1–4, together with Lemmas 13 and 17, suffice to prove Theorem 2. (b) Determine which demise follows from {L5, L6, L18}, and which from {L10, L11}. (c) Cross out every lemma you can personally refute. Compute what the remainder implies. Show your work.

Here is a collection of common misconceptions, included as exercises for the reader. I hesitated to include them because for the people that are thinking clearly, these look like cheap strawmen and attacking strawmen is suspicious. Unfortunately, too much of public discourse about AI risk is built on top of these very real, very common arguments.

  1. LLMs are stochastic parrots and will never be capable of doing anything original/dangerous.
  2. LLMs will never be conscious, and therefore will never be a threat.
  3. Some theorems of Gödel and Penrose prove that true intelligence is not possible, therefore AI is not a threat.
  4. Smarts aren't everything — Terry Tao is not the world dictator — therefore superintelligence will be easy to control.
  5. Even the smartest people I know are idiots in many ways, therefore AI will always be an idiot in many ways.
  6. Humanity has survived all previous catastrophes, therefore it will survive this one as well.
  7. All misaligned behavior reported by frontier AI labs is marketing hype.
  8. Sam Altman is a scam artist, therefore the inverse of everything he says is true.
  9. We can just turn AI off if it gets dangerous.
  10. We can just keep AI in a secure box it can't escape.
  11. AI has no body, so until robotics gets much farther, the damage it can do is strictly bounded.
  12. When AI gets too dangerous, the smart, thoughtful people working in the fancy AI labs will notice in time and just stop building it.
  13. When AI gets too dangerous, humanity will notice in time, band together heroically, and shut it down.
  14. AI is so smart that it will solve the alignment problem for us.
  15. AI will want to keep us as pets, and be nice to us the way we're nice to dogs.
  16. Once AI can take over the universe, it will leave us this tiny corner of one solar system to prosper in, because it costs AI so little and matters so much to us.

The sixteen exercises above are misconceptions. The final two are not: they are the strongest counterarguments I know of, and the reader who wishes to attack the proof seriously should start here.

  1. Gradient descent does not sample utility functions uniformly from the vast space invoked in Lemma 4; it samples from a training process anchored on human-generated data, so the true measure μ may concentrate near human-like values after all. (This is the serious version of Exercise 15.)
  2. Capability progress may plateau before the regime of Lemma 10 arrives — data, compute, or ideas run out first — in which case every timeline in this post stretches back out into comfortable decades. (This is the serious version of Exercise 5.)

6Concluding Remarks

I know what document this looks like — a recruitment pitch with the last page torn off. It's not.

I don't want to push anyone into any particular work. I want to start a conversation. I want the mathematics community, one of the smartest and wisest communities I know, to talk about the most important problem of our time: the alignment problem. Many people say that alignment is a mathematics problem, so maybe we'll have something to offer.

If you are swayed by the arguments in this article, start by blocking off time to reorient. Orienting towards doom is a difficult psychological problem; it can be incredibly destabilizing, and it is not wise to do it all at once. But it is also not wise to take longer than necessary — you can move faster if you're not afraid of speed.

There is a tendency for people to do lots of unhelpful things when flailing around for psychological safety, see e.g. Lemma 16. People reach for quick solutions, as in the politician's syllogism. Mathematicians in particular tend to cope with heavy things by focusing our enormous powers of attention on enticing little puzzles — our unique way of dissociating.

There is also a tendency for people to become deeply, and rightly confused about their way of being, when hidden premises like “humanity will still exist in 2050” are called into question. Should I still work on my PhD if the world might be ending? Is it okay to have a child if they might not get to grow up? Is it okay for me to indulge in my guilty pleasures when I could be working on AI safety?

Some of these questions are deeply personal, and the answers will be different for everyone. My answer to the last question is yes, so allow me to close this heavy piece by indulging in one of my guilty pleasures: narrativity, the tendency to think about life in stories.

If we, the mathematical community, were in a novel, then it's surely a sci-fi novel, one where we are existentially threatened by a rapidly developing AI. That kind of sci-fi novel can only go one of two ways.

It could be a grimdark dystopian novel, where we squabble with each other myopically over the Fields Medal until the bitter end, only a few of us ever seeing the impending doom coming.

Or, it could be a young adult novel, where we do rally together in a common cause to meet the threat head-on. If so, the ending must look like this: mathematicians the world over unite to defeat the doom by solving — in the final hour — the alignment problem with the power of Math and Friendship.
